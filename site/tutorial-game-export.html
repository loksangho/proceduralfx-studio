Your first effect in a game

Tutorial 1 · about 10 minutes

You'll start from a ready-made looping fire, change how it moves, and export it as a sprite sheet: one PNG holding every frame in a grid. Then you'll play it in Bevy, Godot or Unity.

1. Start from a preset

  1. Choose File → New from Preset → Fire (loop). A campfire flame appears, framed by its own camera.
  2. Press Space to play it. The first time, the fire simulates for a few seconds before it plays smoothly.

There are five presets. Fire and Smoke loop. Impact, Magic burst and Slash play once. Each is already set up to export.

2. Change it

  1. In the network editor, double-click the fire node to go inside it. You'll see source (a small sphere the fire burns from), fire_sim (the Pyro simulation) and fire_loop (which makes it loop).
  2. Click fire_sim. Its settings appear in the Parameters panel.
  3. Raise Turbulence for a wilder flame, or Buoyancy to make it rise faster. Lower Burn rate for a smaller fire. The simulation restarts after each change.
  4. Press U to come back up.

3. Export the sprite sheet

  1. Choose Render → Export Sprite Sheet….
  2. Leave Frame size at 192 × 256 for now. Trim crops away the empty space around the flame, and Seamless makes the end of the loop lead into the start.
  3. Click Preview to play the frames exactly as they'll export. If the flame is cut off at an edge, the app says so. Make the frame bigger or move the camera back.
  4. Under Also for, tick Godot or Unity if that's your engine. Bevy needs nothing extra.
  5. Click Export… and save it as fire.png inside your game's project. Beside it you get fire.pfx.json, which describes the grid and frame rate.

4a. Play it in Bevy

Add the free bevy_procedural_fx plugin (cargo add bevy_procedural_fx), put fire.png and fire.pfx.json in assets/fx/, and spawn it:

use bevy::prelude::*;
use bevy_procedural_fx::{FxSprite, ProceduralFxPlugin};

fn main() {
    App::new()
        .add_plugins((DefaultPlugins, ProceduralFxPlugin))
        .add_systems(Startup, setup)
        .run();
}

fn setup(mut commands: Commands, assets: Res<AssetServer>) {
    commands.spawn(Camera2d);
    commands.spawn(FxSprite::looping(assets.load("fx/fire.pfx.json")));
}

4b. Play it in Godot 4

  1. Export into your Godot project's folder, with Godot ticked. Beside the PNG you get fire.tres, a SpriteFrames resource.
  2. Add an AnimatedSprite2D node. In the Inspector, drag fire.tres onto Sprite Frames.
  3. Turn on Autoplay on Load for the default animation, using the button in the SpriteFrames panel at the bottom, or call $AnimatedSprite2D.play(). It loops at the sheet's frame rate.

4c. Play it in Unity

  1. Export into your Unity project's Assets folder, with Unity ticked. Unity imports the PNG already sliced into one sprite per frame (fire_0, fire_1…), full size and uncompressed.
  2. In the Project window, open fire.png's arrow, select all the sprites, and drag them into the scene. Unity asks where to save a new animation, then plays it.
  3. Unity picks the new clip's frame rate itself. To play at the sheet's own rate (24 for this fire), set the clip's sample rate in the Animation window.

5. For particle systems

Particle systems play every cell of the grid, so tick Fill the grid under Particles. The sheet then gets a layout with no empty cells: 24 frames make 6 × 4, not 5 × 5 with one blank.

  • Godot: with Godot ticked too, you also get fire_particles.tres. Set it as a GPUParticles2D's material, and the PNG as its texture. The process material's Animation → Speed sets how fast the frames go: 1 plays them once over each particle's life.
  • Unity: in the Particle System, turn on Texture Sheet Animation. Use Grid mode with the columns and rows from the export (they're in fire.pfx.json), or Sprites mode with the sliced sprites.

6. Fewer frames, still smooth: motion vectors

A sheet with fewer frames is smaller and loads faster, but plays jerkily, especially slowed down. Tick Motion vectors under Smooth playback and the export also writes fire_motion.png: how each pixel moves on its way to the next frame. The game then slides each frame towards the next instead of jumping, so a sheet of every 4th frame (set Frames to every 4) can play about as smoothly as the full one.

  • Bevy: spawn FxFlipbook::looping(assets.load("fx/fire.pfx.json")) instead of FxSprite (bevy_procedural_fx 0.3).
  • Godot: with Godot ticked, drag fire_flipbook.tscn into your scene. It's a Sprite2D with a shader that does the sliding.
  • Unity: with Unity ticked, put fire_flipbook.mat on a Quad, and scale the Quad to the frame's shape. It works with URP and the built-in pipeline.

It works best for smoke, explosions and anything that moves smoothly. Fast, scattered sparks jump further between frames than the gaps between them, so for those, keep more frames.

7. Light it

Tick Normal map under Lighting, and the export also writes fire_normal.png: each frame's shape, read from how opaque it is. Your game's 2D lights then light the effect from their side, so smoke looks like a soft puff rather than a flat picture. It suits smoke, dust and clouds; leave glowing effects such as fire unlit.

  • Bevy: spawn FxLight2d lights. Every FxFlipbook whose sheet has a normal map is lit by them (bevy_procedural_fx 0.3).
  • Godot: with Godot ticked, put fire_sprite.tres on the AnimatedSprite2D as its material, and add a PointLight2D. The flipbook scene is lit too. Raise the light's Height to light the front more.
  • Unity: with Unity ticked, the sliced sprites already carry the normal map. With URP's 2D Renderer, use the Sprite-Lit-Default material and add a Light 2D.

8. Make it glow

A flame's core is brighter than a PNG can hold, so the sheet rolls it off to white, and a game's bloom can't tell it from white smoke. Tick Glow sheet under Glow, and the export also writes fire_glow.png: the glow itself, at any brightness. Your game puts it back, at full strength or stronger, and its bloom makes the hot parts glow. The sheet itself is unchanged, so it still works without it.

  • Bevy: FxFlipbook adds it (.with_glow(2.0) for more). Put Hdr and Bloom on the camera.
  • Godot: with Godot ticked, fire_sprite.tres (for the AnimatedSprite2D) and the flipbook scene add it, with a glow_strength. Turn on HDR 2D in Project Settings → Rendering → Viewport, and glow in a WorldEnvironment.
  • Unity: with Unity and Motion vectors ticked, set the flipbook material's Glow strength. Turn on HDR and Bloom.

9. Keep it updating while your game runs

Tick Keep updating under Live before you export. From then on, the app exports again a moment after every change. A Bevy game run with Bevy's file_watcher feature shows the change while it runs. Godot and Unity pick up the new files when you switch back to their window.

Share it

To post an effect, click Flipbook under the timeline. When it's recorded, the flipbook viewer's Save GIF… saves a looping GIF. From a terminal, proceduralfx_studio animation fire.pfs fire.gif does the same in one step.
